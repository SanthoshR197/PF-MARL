**II. Related Work**

The existing literature on using machine learning for epidemic control covers several interconnected domains, such as reinforcement learning for policy decisions, multi-agent cooperation, simulation-based disease modeling, and privacy-protecting distributed learning. To systematically position the proposed work, prior studies are categorized into four key directions: (i) reinforcement learning approaches for epidemic control, (ii) multi-agent and advanced reinforcement learning frameworks, (iii) simulation and hybrid epidemiological modeling techniques, and (iv) privacy and federated learning-based methods. This structured categorization enables a clear understanding of the capabilities and limitations of existing approaches and highlights the gap addressed by the proposed PF-MARL framework.


**A. Reinforcement Learning for Epidemic Control**

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a promising paradigm for improving intervention plans in disease outbreaks because it is well-suited to sequential decision-making under uncertain conditions. Early research mainly focused on finding optimal policies within centralized epidemic modeling frameworks.

Libin et al. [1] developed a deep RL system operating on a large-scale SEIR model with hundreds of interconnected regions. Their method used policy gradient techniques to learn effective mitigation strategies such as school closure policies, demonstrating that coordination between districts leads to better health outcomes. However, the training process operates from a single central point and does not support decentralized decision-making or incorporate privacy-preserving mechanisms.

Similarly, Bushaj et al. [3] developed a Simulation-Deep Reinforcement Learning (SiRL) setup that combines agent-based epidemic modeling with a deep RL decision-maker. This method allows testing of various interventions including vaccine allocation and policy restrictions using a reward system that measures both health outcomes and economic effects. Nevertheless, the system depends on a single central agent, does not support coordination between different geographic areas, and assumes all regions follow uniform rules, which may not reflect real-world heterogeneity.

Khatami and Gopalappa [4] expanded RL-based epidemic control by incorporating interactions between multiple jurisdictions. Their model treats epidemic management as a Markov Decision Process and employs deep Q-networks to optimize lockdown plans that balance the reduction of infections, deaths, and economic costs. The model accounts for travel patterns to capture inter-regional dynamics; however, it is limited to smaller regions and does not scale to more complex, large-scale settings.

Ohi et al. [2] explored the use of deep reinforcement learning to derive optimal epidemic mitigation strategies within a simulated environment that jointly models disease dynamics and economic impact. Their approach trains a Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN) agent in a stochastic SEIR-inspired virtual environment where population movement and social interactions govern infection spread. The agent selects from multiple intervention levels, including social distancing and lockdowns, guided by a reward function that simultaneously accounts for active case counts, mortality, and economic performance. The learned policy exhibits an adaptive behavior, initially enforcing prolonged lockdowns to curb early infection surges and subsequently transitioning to cyclic, shorter interventions to manage successive waves while preserving economic activity. Notably, the agent's decisions are informed not only by current infection levels but also by epidemiological indicators such as the reproduction rate, enabling more context-aware and temporally responsive control strategies. Despite these strengths, the framework remains confined to a single-region, centralized setting and does not address the challenges of multi-jurisdictional coordination or privacy-preserving data aggregation.

Overall, these approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of reinforcement learning in epidemic control. Nevertheless, they predominantly rely on centralized learning paradigms, do not support scalable coordination among many agents, and do not incorporate privacy-preserving mechanisms, limiting their utility in real-world large-scale deployments where data privacy is a critical concern.


**B. Multi-Agent and Advanced Reinforcement Learning Approaches**

To overcome the limitations of centralized reinforcement learning, recent studies have explored advanced formulations incorporating multi-agent coordination, hierarchical decision-making, and graph-based learning to better model the distributed and interconnected nature of epidemic systems.

The H2-MARL framework proposed by Luo et al. [5] is a multi-agent reinforcement learning framework designed to achieve Pareto optimality between conflicting objectives. It instantiates separate agents for each administrative area and employs a dual-goal reward system to jointly optimize health outcomes and economic impacts over time. The framework is coupled with a spatiotemporal epidemic simulator built on a dynamic SIHR model and evaluated using a large human mobility dataset containing over one billion records from various cities. Results demonstrate reductions in hospital strain and mobility restriction disruptions across different urban settings. However, the system requires centralized training and full access to private health and movement data, making it difficult to deploy in environments where privacy regulations restrict data sharing.

Du et al. [6] proposed HRL4EC, a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework for managing outbreaks across multiple control modes. It decomposes complex intervention plans into layered decision-making steps and employs a novel epidemiological model, MID-SEIR, to explicitly represent the effects of various actions on infection spread. By restructuring the outbreak management challenge hierarchically, the system simplifies the decision space and supports the simultaneous optimization of multiple interventions. Evaluations on both real and synthetic data demonstrate superior performance compared to single-intervention methods.

Hurtado and Marculescu [7] proposed a graph-based system for multi-agent reinforcement learning that integrates Graph Neural Networks with MARL to manage disease spread in a decentralized manner. The method forecasts infection probabilities at specific locations using real mobility data and deploys multiple agents to adjust movement behavior accordingly. By exploiting both spatial and mobility patterns, the framework suppresses the reproduction number below critical thresholds without imposing hard lockdowns. Despite its strong alignment with real-world urban dynamics, the framework assumes access to detailed individual-level mobility data and does not incorporate formal privacy guarantees or federated learning mechanisms.

These approaches are promising in modeling how diseases spread across networks and in supporting coordinated multi-agent responses. However, most rely on a centralized data source, lack integration with federated learning paradigms, and do not provide rigorous privacy guarantees, thereby limiting their deployment in real-world, privacy-sensitive urban environments.


**C. Simulation and Epidemiological Modeling Techniques**

Simulation-based and epidemiological modeling approaches play a crucial role in understanding disease transmission dynamics and evaluating intervention strategies. These methods provide structured representations of population interactions, mobility patterns, and disease progression, forming the foundation for intelligent decision-making frameworks.

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has been widely used to capture fine-grained epidemic dynamics by representing individuals as independent entities with unique behaviors. Cuevas [8] proposed an agent-based framework to assess COVID-19 transmission risks within facilities, in which each agent follows behavioral rules governed by individual characteristics and spatial interactions. This approach enables the modeling of diverse populations and accurate micro-level transmission patterns. Similarly, Truszkowska et al. [11] developed a high-resolution agent-based model capable of replicating disease spread at the individual level in a real urban environment. Their model incorporates specific demographic, spatial, and behavioral characteristics, enabling precise evaluation of intervention tactics such as testing and immunization.

Network-based epidemiological approaches, in contrast, focus on interactions between aggregated population groups rather than individuals. Humphries et al. [9] presented a metapopulation network model in which regions are represented as nodes connected by mobility links. Each node tracks compartmental epidemic dynamics while inter-node connections capture travel and commuting patterns, providing a natural graph-based representation for large-scale disease transmission across geographically dispersed regions.

The significance of human mobility in shaping transmission dynamics has further been demonstrated by mobility-driven epidemic models. Iyaniwura et al. [10] proposed a hybrid gravity-metapopulation model that incorporates spatial distance and mobility data into the transmission process, enabling assessment of how interregional movement affects infection rates across time-dependent mobility patterns.

Overall, these studies offer complementary perspectives on epidemic spread, ranging from individual-level simulation to network-scale modeling. However, most frameworks are not designed to support adaptive, learning-based policy generation, and few incorporate privacy-friendly distributed decision-making. These limitations motivate the need for frameworks that unify simulation fidelity, multi-agent coordination, and adaptive policy learning, as addressed by the proposed PF-MARL system.


**D. Privacy-Preserving and Federated Learning Approaches**

The increasing reliance on data-driven epidemic control systems raises concerns about privacy, security, and distributed coordination. Epidemic data is inherently sensitive, geographically distributed, and subject to strict regulatory constraints. Consequently, traditional centralized learning approaches that require raw data aggregation are often impractical. To address these limitations, recent research has explored federated learning and privacy-preserving reinforcement learning frameworks.

Federated learning enables distributed groups to collaboratively train a shared model without exchanging raw data, preserving the confidentiality of local information while still enabling cross-site learning. Zhou et al. [13] developed a system using federated reinforcement learning for epidemic decision-making by combining regional data without exposing sensitive information. Their results demonstrate that this approach accelerates decision convergence and outperforms single-region models, particularly when data is scarce and sensitive. However, the approach retains a largely centralized coordination mechanism and does not fully address scalability and inter-regional heterogeneity.

Antunes et al. [14] examine the applicability of federated learning in healthcare, demonstrating that it supports machine learning on sensitive data such as electronic health records. By training models locally at each site and transmitting only model parameters, the method preserves patient privacy and complies with regulatory constraints. Despite its advantages, federated learning faces persistent challenges including communication overhead, data heterogeneity, and vulnerability to model inversion attacks.

To improve scalability and robustness, decentralized federated learning approaches have been proposed. Guan et al. [15] developed a decentralized model grounded in population movement patterns, in which nodes exchange updates directly without relying on a central server. Their system employs a mobility-based adjacency matrix to define inter-regional connectivity while preserving local data isolation. This structure is particularly relevant for epidemic modeling, where disease spread is inherently shaped by mobility-driven interactions.

In parallel, privacy-preserving distributed learning methods aim to protect both data and model information during collaborative training. Froelicher et al. [16] introduced SPINDLE, a system that enables multi-site learning using homomorphic encryption, allowing participants to train jointly without sharing sensitive information. This method protects both data and model parameters, even under adversarial conditions, while achieving performance comparable to centralized training. However, cryptographic methods of this nature introduce significant computational overhead and are difficult to integrate with adaptive reinforcement learning frameworks.

Differential privacy offers a formal mechanism for protecting individual data during model training. Yang-Zhao and Ng [12] studied privacy-preserving reinforcement learning within population models by injecting calibrated noise into state and reward inputs to satisfy formal privacy standards. Their work demonstrates that a favorable trade-off between privacy and utility is achievable, particularly in large-scale population settings. However, applying differential privacy in reinforcement learning introduces additional challenges, including increased stochasticity and potential degradation in policy performance.

Overall, although substantial progress has been made across federated learning, distributed systems, and differential privacy individually, these components are predominantly studied in isolation. Existing methods either emphasize federated coordination without rigorous privacy guarantees, or address privacy-preserving mechanisms without incorporating adaptive multi-agent decision-making. Furthermore, the combined challenges of scalability, communication efficiency, and decentralized policy learning remain largely unaddressed.

These limitations motivate the development of unified frameworks that integrate federated learning, multi-agent reinforcement learning, and differential privacy into a cohesive system. The proposed PF-MARL framework closes this gap by enabling decentralized, multi-region policy optimization with strong formal privacy guarantees and efficient distributed coordination for large-scale epidemic control.

Table 1 illustrates the major merits and demerits of different approaches in reinforcement learning, multi-agent systems, epidemiological modeling, and privacy-preserving distributed learning. Even though reinforcement learning methods can highly optimize intervention strategies, most of the time, they are focused on centralized settings only and do not have the ability to coordinate multiple regions on a large scale. Multi-agent reinforcement learning approaches mitigate this problem to some extent by allowing distributed decision-making; however, they typically assume unrestricted access to sensitive data and do not incorporate formal privacy guarantees.

Simulation-based and epidemiological models offer an accurate depiction of disease spread and human mobility but have hardly changed their operation to make the policy actually be the result of a learning process. On the other hand, federated learning and privacy-preserving methods solve the issue of data control by a single party through decentralized training and a formal privacy guarantee however their operation is quite the opposite to that of sequential decision-making or coordinated control in the course of a dynamic epidemic environments.

As a result, existing approaches fail to simultaneously achieve scalable multi-agent coordination, adaptive policy learning, and rigorous privacy preservation within a unified framework. This limitation motivates the proposed PF-MARL system, which integrates multi-agent reinforcement learning with federated learning and differential privacy to enable decentralized, privacy-aware, and dynamically optimized epidemic control across interconnected urban regions.
