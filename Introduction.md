# I. Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic's unparalleled scope and speed have brought to light the weaknesses in the world's urban infrastructure, highlighting the critical conflict between maintaining economic stability and protecting public health. Health authorities are often forced to make binary, centralised decisions during severe outbreaks; these decisions frequently oscillate between relaxed mobility policies that prioritise the economy but cause uncontrollable viral surges, and total economic lockdowns that guarantee pandemic control at significant financial cost [1], [2]. This problem is primarily caused by the incapacity of traditional administrative structures to coordinate heterogeneous, granular interventions across interdependent urban districts.

By simulating sequential decision-making under uncertainty, recent developments in Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) have opened up promising paths for optimising epidemic mitigation strategies [2], [3]. Current epidemic models frequently use agent-based simulations or interconnected graphs to depict human mobility and disease transmission [9], [10]. However, there are three significant obstacles to overcome when integrating AI-driven continuous control with actual public health infrastructure. First, centralised modelling paradigms are often used by traditional RL frameworks [1], which are inadequate for capturing the dynamic heterogeneity of various urban networks (e.g., residential vs. commercial districts). Second, although Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) permits decentralised execution [5], [7], agents interacting in epidemic environments require intricate coordination to prevent localised policies from causing cross-border infection spillover. Lastly, and most critically, gathering the systemic joint-state information required to train these cooperative models typically demands the centralisation of highly sensitive demographic, mobility, and medical data, directly violating international data privacy regulations and principles of institutional data sovereignty [12], [14].

To address these interrelated issues, this paper proposes the **Privacy-Preserving Federated Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (PF-MARL)** framework, a novel hierarchical learning architecture designed for scalable, decentralised, and privacy-secure epidemic control. Based on local SEIR-M (Susceptible-Exposed-Infected-Recovered-Mobility) infection dynamics, the proposed system enables localised edge clients (representing distinct urban districts) to independently optimise non-pharmaceutical interventions, including mobility restrictions, testing quotas, and social distancing enforcement.

In contrast to earlier models that necessitate centralised data pools, PF-MARL employs a Centralised Training with Decentralised Execution (CTDE) regime supported by an $(\epsilon, \delta)$-Differential Privacy (DP) layer and a robust Federated Learning (FL) protocol. During training, localised Critic networks evaluate joint-action consequences and inject calibrated noise to scrub gradients, thereby preventing the memorisation of distinct medical records. Because native DP noise inherently destabilises multi-agent policy gradients, this paper presents a novel *Hook-Free Critic Isolation* method to preserve training stability. The FedAvg algorithm is then used to aggregate the scrubbed parameters via a Federated Hub [13], creating a globally optimal pandemic strategy without transmitting raw data.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
1. **Hierarchical Privacy-Preserving Framework:** We introduce the PF-MARL architecture, which seamlessly integrates MARL, Differential Privacy, and Federated Learning across three tiers to orchestrate cooperative urban pandemic containment without centralised data collection.
2. **Hook-Free Critic Isolation Strategy:** To resolve the inherent training instability caused by DP noise in multi-agent environments, we propose a weight-synchronized isolation mechanism that decouples noisy value estimations from stable policy gradients.
3. **Multi-Objective Pareto Optimality:** We demonstrate that PF-MARL achieves an optimal multi-objective equilibrium, suppressing peak infection rates by 49.46% (approaching total lockdown efficacy) while preserving 52.6% of baseline economic capacity (a 7x improvement over total closures).
4. **Quantification of the Privacy Tax:** We rigorously evaluate the utility-privacy trade-off, demonstrating that enforcing mathematical data sovereignty ($\epsilon = 2.0, \delta = 10^{-5}$) incurs only a manageable 11.4-unit reduction in economic efficiency, making the system highly practical for real-world deployment.
