IV. Results and Discussion
A. Learning Efficiency and Convergence Analysis
The ability of the system to find the best pandemic management policies even when very strict privacy constraints were in place was measured over a training horizon of 5,000 episodes (Fig. 1). This study is an indicator of the technical robustness of the framework when it is subjected to differential privacy perturbations.
Reward Convergence and Policy Acquisition: The top panel of Fig. 1 shows the change of the cumulative moving average reward, which is a dual-objective of health preservation and economic stability. Starting from an initial stochastic state of -25.0, the Reward gradually converges to its steady-state level of -17.6.
This result is a clear indication of how well the Hook-Free Isolation Strategy works. Since the noisy critic updates from the privacy-preserving noise injection are separated from the policy-gradient, the agents are able to learn the strategy of maximizing the utility, which is usually unstable due to privacy-preserving noise injection.
Epidemiological Equilibrium: The bottom panel (Epidemic Control) tracks the systemic infection levels throughout the training period. After the first exploratory phase, the overall infected population in the urban network reduces drastically and stays at a resilient equilibrium of 20 people per time step.
This equilibrium implies the learned coordinated global policy was so effective as to "crush the curve." The absence of any resurgence during the last 3,000 episodes demonstrates the long-term effectiveness of the PF-MARL approach in keeping a low-infection steady state in spite of different district scenarios.

B. Comparative Performance Evaluation
The PF-MARL framework was compared to five other widely used control methods in order to show that it is better: (i) Centralized MADDPG (without privacy constraints), (ii) Static Lockdown (complete closure of the city), (iii) No-Action (free movement), (iv) FedMADDPG (No-DP) (federated but without differential privacy), and (v) Random Allocation (uncoordinated actions).
1) Economic Resilience Analysis: Each strategy's effect on the efficiency of urban economic activities is depicted in the Economic Resilience Index (Fig. 2). The PF-MARL scheme sustains a level of economic activity that is 52.6% of the pre-pandemic period. On the other hand, a conventional Total Lockdown policy would shrink economic activity to just 7.5%. This 7x improvement in economic resilience demonstrates that the proposed AI-driven policy is capable of keeping over half of the city's business infrastructure operational while still managing the crisis. This validates the "Economic Stability" objective of the research.
2) Infection Suppression Comparison (Fig. 3): The ability of each strategy to slow down virus transmission was measured mainly by looking at the highest infection numbers during the trials. The urban network under the No-Action scenario, shows a quick infection peak of 358.22 individuals. With the PF-MARL system, this peak is only 181.04 individuals.
This represents a reduction of 49.46% in the highest number of infections than without any control measures. Also, the PF-MARL method gave 32.08% better results in lowering infections than the Random Allocation method (266.50 individuals), demonstrating that the learned collaboration between districts is mathematically a better approach compared to uncoordinated health responses. The negligible 0.05% utility gap between the PF-MARL (Private) and the Centralized MADDPG (Non-Private) models confirms that our system achieves near-optimal pandemic suppression without risking the exposure of sensitive health records.

C. Multi-Objective Trade-off and Pareto Optimality
The effectiveness of the PF-MARL framework is evaluated through a multi-objective lens in Fig. 4 (Pareto Analysis). This scatter plot depicts the trade-off between two objectives: minimizing the number of infected individuals and minimizing the cost of interventions. In this coordinate system, the "Optimal Direction" is bottom-left meaning no epidemic and no epidemic disruption.
1) Boundary extremes of static baselines: Standard methods are stuck at the far ends of the objective space, as illustrated by Fig. 4. The No-Action scenario (Red Diamond) keeps the intervention costs at a minimum (10.0 units) but leads to a dangerously large infection increase of 244.6 individuals.
However, the Total Lockdown approach (Grey Triangle) can substantially limit the number of infected individuals to 41.0, but at the same time, it suffers the largest economic loss of 60.1 units. Both of these baseline cases essentially serve as non-ideal "extreme points" solutions which ignore the trade-off between saving the urban economy and ensuring public health.
PF-MARL as a Pareto-Dominant Equilibrium: The proposed PF-MARL framework (Green Circle) occupies the central region of the Pareto space, representing a superior multi-objective compromise. The proposed system reaches steady state with an average of 160.2 infections and an economic cost of 31.1 units. By moving the policy very near the optimal origin in comparison to a total lockdown, the framework delivers a 48.25% decrease in economic costs, while the infection level is still kept at a manageable steady state. Such a trade-off in performance indeed shows the ability of our framework to figure out rather complex, district-wise targeting policies that can beat narrow, city-wide mandates. Besides, the closeness of the private model (Green) to the non-private one (Orange) indicates that privacy-preserving methods do not cause a major loss of the essential Pareto-optimalness of the policy obtained.

(ϵ,δ)-Differential Privacy feature. This is quantified by contrasting the private PF-MARL framework with the FedMADDPG (non-private) baseline.

δ=10−5, the non-private model achieves an optimal economic cost of 19.7 units, whereas the private PF-MARL model incurs a cost of 31.1 units. This represents a utility gap of 11.4 units in overall economic efficiency, directly attributable to the gradient perturbations enforced by the (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon, \delta)
(ϵ,δ)-Differential Privacy layer.

Health Aspect: Interestingly, the overall infection rate remains almost steady, even the private model attains a slightly lower average of 160.2 infections whereas the non-private baseline is 167.3.
2) Examination of the "Privacy Tax": The findings reveal that the major price of incorporating differential privacy is not health safety loss, but the reduction in economic accuracy. The random noise introduced to the gradients makes the district agents take more 'cautious' policy. Due to the fact privacy noise prevents agents from accurately mapping the economic-utility landscape, they end up with health interventions that are more aggressive (and therefore more expensive). Such a large 11.4 units efficiency gap is considered a legitimate "Deployment Tax." As the non-private baseline involves a centralization of sensitive patient data (which is legally and ethically prohibited), the PF-MARL method is practically the only solution for urban real-world networks. Giving up slightly economic efficient is not a big loss if one in turn gains mathematically data sovereignty over the health city infrastructure.

E. Architectural Ablation Study
An ablation study was carried out to test how each part affects system performance by removing the key components one by one. This helped to measure stability, learning progress, and policy usefulness. And the results showed which parts were must-have.
1) Ablation: Without Differential Privacy (FedMADDPG Baseline)
Hypothesis: Evaluating the trade-off introduced by privacy-preserving mechanisms.
Result: As analyzed in Section IV.D, removing the (ϵ,δ)(\epsilon,\delta)
(ϵ,δ)-Differential Privacy layer (orange baseline in Fig. 4) eliminates gradient perturbation, resulting in an improvement of 11.4 units in economic efficiency. Yet, this non-private setup weakens data security and isn't practical for real healthcare environments. The ablation shows the DP layer protects data privacy and causes only a limited drop in economic results, making the opacus-based privacy engine a viable choice. This outcome aligns with operational constraints in medical systems.

2) Ablation: Without Federated Learning (Centralized Baseline)
Hypothesis: Evaluating whether centralized learning outperforms decentralized coordination.
Result: In the centralized configuration (blue baseline in Fig. 4), all district-level data flow into one global model. Even with complete access to all data, the central model shows a higher average infection rate (175.7 infections) compared to the proposed PF-MARL framework (160.2 infections).
3) Ablation: Without Critic-Clone Isolation ("Hook-Free" Removal)
Hypothesis: Evaluating the necessity of the proposed critic isolation mechanism under differential privacy constraints.
Result: In preliminary experiments, standard MADDPG configurations attempted to propagate actor gradients through a critic network subjected to differential privacy-induced noise via the Opacus engine. This configuration resulted in severe training instability and failure to converge under strict privacy budgets (ϵ=2.0\epsilon=2.0
ϵ=2.0). The gradient noise damaged the quality of policy adjustments. Making agents make poor choices during key decisions.

This test shows that the Hook-Free Critic Isolation method, which uses a clone of the critic for actor updates, is necessary for stable learning. Without it, multi-agent training and privacy protection can't work together. Policy fine-tuning stays unaffected by the noisy value estimates in the critic.
